The Filter: Why Jane Street Is So Hard to Get Into

Walk through the hiring process as an applicant and see where you get stopped.

Jane Street is one of the most selective places a finance student can apply. My research paper asks why: what hiring practices and candidate qualifications make a trading role there so hard to land? I read two groups of researchers who almost never cite each other. Sociologist Lauren Rivera studies how elite firms actually hire. Industrial-organizational psychologists study how firms should hire if they want to pick the people who will do the job well. I think each group explains a different barrier, and for a student at a school like UB, the first barrier is the bigger one.

I could just say that in an essay, but I wanted you to go through it yourself. The page has three gates. At each one you make a choice, and then you see what the research says usually happens next.

Gate 1 of 3 · The résumé screen

Who gets seen at all

Imagine you are the screener. You have a stack of applications and a few seconds for each. Pick the one candidate you would move forward.

Rivera interviewed 120 people who make hiring decisions at elite banks, law firms and consulting firms, and she watched one firm's recruiting from the inside. In her 2011 study she found that screeners sort résumés by school prestige first. Firms recruit mainly from a short list of "target" schools, and applicants from elsewhere often get set aside before anyone weighs their skills. Screeners also gave weight to polished, expensive extracurriculars, the kind that signal a certain upbringing more than a certain ability.

Source: Rivera (2011)

For you, at UBThis is the gate that hurts most. Your skills may never reach a human evaluator because the first filter is the name of your school. That is why referrals, competitions and reaching out to people directly matter so much for you. They get your name in front of someone without going through the screen.
For you, at a target schoolYou likely clear this gate on the school name alone. Firms come to your campus, and your résumé lands on the "read" pile by default. That helps a lot, and you didn't have to show any skill to get it.
Gate 2 of 3 · The technical interview

Can you think in probabilities, fast?

Quantitative trading interviews are known for probability, expected value and mental math under time pressure. Here is a question in that style that I wrote myself. The clock is already running.

Time: 0:00

You roll one fair six-sided die and win that many dollars. Before you take the money you may reroll once, but then you must keep the second roll. Playing well, what is this game worth?

Show the reasoning

A single roll is worth $3.50 on average. So keep any first roll above 3.50 (a 4, 5 or 6, average $5) and reroll a 1, 2 or 3 (worth $3.50). Half the time you get $5 and half the time $3.50, so the game is worth $4.25.

Once a candidate is in the room, the psychology research takes over. Sackett and colleagues (2022) re-ran decades of hiring studies and corrected an error that had inflated older estimates. Their result surprised the field. Structured interviews, where every candidate gets the same questions and the same scoring rubric, predict job performance best. General cognitive ability tests are still useful but weaker than long believed.

Nye and colleagues (2022) add a second piece. Specific mental abilities, like quantitative reasoning, predict performance beyond general intelligence. A firm that tests narrow, job-related skills in a fixed format is using the methods with the best research behind them. So this gate is hard on purpose. It measures the skill and very little else, which means prep and ability decide who gets through.

Sources: Sackett et al. (2022); Nye et al. (2022)

For you, at UBThis gate is hard for everyone, and that is partly good news for you. A structured test of specific skills doesn't care much about your school's name. Once you're in the room, you're competing on closer to even terms than at Gate 1.
For you, at a target schoolYour school name stops helping here. A structured technical interview only scores your answer. Plenty of people who got through Gate 1 easily get stuck here.
Gate 3 of 3 · Fit

Does charm still count?

Same candidate, two kinds of interview. Switch between them.

What gets asked

Whatever the interviewer feels like. "Walk me through your résumé." "What do you do for fun?" Conversation drifts toward shared hobbies.

What gets rewarded

Chemistry. Rivera found interviewers favor people who remind them of themselves: similar backgrounds, similar pastimes, similar style. "Fit" can decide the offer.

What gets asked

The same job-related questions, in the same order, for every candidate. Answers are scored against a rubric written in advance.

What gets rewarded

The quality of the answer. Levashina and colleagues found structure reduces bias and makes charm and self-promotion less effective.

In her 2012 study, Rivera showed that "cultural matching" shapes hiring at elite firms. The evaluators she interviewed wanted people they would personally enjoy working with. Still, Rivera herself notes that fit matters less when a job is highly technical. Levashina and colleagues (2014) explain why: structured formats limit the room for a gut feeling to override the evidence. Trading is about as technical as finance jobs get, so my guess is that fit is the weakest of the three gates at a firm like Jane Street. I can't prove that from my sources, though, since none of them studied a trading firm.

Sources: Rivera (2012); Levashina et al. (2014)

From UB, your biggest problem is getting a person to look at your résumé at all. The later gates are hard, but the research suggests they mostly judge what you can do. The first one mostly judges where you go to school.

Jane Street is hard to get into for two reasons: the interviews test your skill, and the résumé screen tests your school.

Rivera explains the pipeline that filters out a lot of applicants before anyone sees what they can do. The psychologists explain why the interviews after that are so hard to pass: they use the methods that best predict how someone will do on the job. Put the two together and "hard to get into" means something different depending on where you start.

The counterpoint: maybe hard is fair

The best argument against me is that a hard process is a good process. If structured interviews on specific skills really are the best predictors we have, then a firm using them is hiring well. Levashina's review even suggests structure makes hiring less biased. From that angle, the technical gate is the fairest part of the whole process.

I agree with that about Gate 2. Where I disagree is Gate 1, because a fair test only helps the people who get to take it. If the résumé screen leans on school prestige the way Rivera found, then a careful, evidence-based interview sits behind a door that opens wider for some students than others.

My evidence has limits too. Sackett et al. is my strongest source because it rests on decades of studies and shows its math, but some researchers have pushed back on its correction. Rivera offers rare inside access, but she reports what evaluators say rather than measuring how often each factor decides an offer, and she studied banks, law firms and consultancies. The reviews are only as good as the studies they combine, and few of those come from finance. None of my sources studied a quantitative trading firm directly, and that is the gap my final project tries to fill.

Why I made this a website

Our class keeps asking what changes when an argument moves from paper to new media. My answer is that the reader gets to take part. An essay goes in a straight line and asks you to trust the writer. A site like this gives you a choice, lets you change it, and shows you what happens. When you picked a résumé at Gate 1, you were briefly the person Rivera studied. If you picked the Princeton candidate, you felt how natural that shortcut is. If you picked the UB candidate, you saw what that shortcut costs someone like me.

The school buttons at the top are the most important part of the page. The research and the three gates stay the same, but your starting point changes what they mean for you. A paper could say "outcomes depend on your school." Here you can switch schools and watch your result change.

This format has downsides too. Clicking through can feel like a game and make the evidence seem more certain than it is, so I put a source label under every claim. The die question is mine, written in the style of trading interviews, not a real Jane Street question.
